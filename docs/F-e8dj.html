Peter Lombard, Commentarium in Psalmos (Magna glosatura), on Ps 129:3-8 and the incipit of Ps 130; PL 191, cols. 1168-1170

Two pieces of one leaf (a strip and a larger piece on each face, the strips photographed upside down) carry the commentary on Ps 129:3-4 and 6-8 with red-underlined lemmata and red source signs, ending with the red initial of Ps 130. A research reader compared the verse 4 and verse 6 passages with the PL 191 page images (archive.org, cols. 1169-1170) and found agreement in extracts, order and Lombard's own linking phrases. An independent checker compared the lower lines of the canvas 1 piece (Ps 129:7-8 into Ps 130) with the PL page image and confirmed it, adding the word order 'totius peccata [mundi]' and a red 'c' at 'sanguis pretiosus'. Differences include 'Qua spe clamas', 'Unde subdit', 'offerebantur' (all three also in the Glossa ordinaria), 'usquequo moriamur' without 'non', and an ending of verse 8 shorter than PL, where two rubbed lines leave it open whether a closing Cassiodorus sentence was shortened or missing. Compared with the printed edition only. Gilbert of Poitiers' unprinted commentary was not compared, and there is no human review.

Readings and comparison details

F-e8dj: Peter Lombard, Commentarium in Psalmos, on Psalm 129:3-8 and the opening of Psalm 130

The two small parchment pieces of this record come from one leaf of Peter Lombard's commentary on the Psalms, the "Magna glosatura". The text runs from the commentary on Psalm 129:3 (Si iniquitates observaveris) to verse 8, and ends with the red initial and opening words of Psalm 130, Domine non est exaltatum. The verse words commented on (the lemmata) are underlined in red. The commentary is a chain of extracts from Augustine, Cassiodorus and Alcuin, and red marginal signs ("ac", "c", "h") mark their sources. The text follows the printed edition in Migne, Patrologia Latina 191, cols. 1168-1170, with small differences of wording listed below.

The Fragmentarium record describes Stams, Stiftsarchiv, Frg. 146: parchment, two items, 115 x 94 mm (measured on item 146_1, 30/115 x 63/94 mm), with title "x". It gives no catalogue date and no linked description, and it names no text. The reconstruction summary is by Claudia Sojer. The two images, Stams_STA_Frg_146_01r02r and Stams_STA_Frg_146_01v02v, each show both items:

  • a narrow strip, photographed upside down;
  • a larger piece with a small tab below it.

The description fits what the images show. The hand is a small late Caroline or early gothic book hand in one column, with red underlining and red source signs.

  • Identification: identified, high. The text is Peter Lombard, Commentarium in Psalmos, Ps 129:3-8 and the incipit of Ps 130 (PL 191, 1168-1170).
    • Diagnostic basis: on both faces the commentary agrees with Lombard's text in its selection and order of extracts and in his own linking phrases. Examples are "[...] ita iunge. Nullus potest", "In hoc est mihi spes quia apud te est p[ropitiatio]", the Alcuin passage "[va]let quia qui perseveraverit usque in finem hic salu[us erit] ... in lege caput et cauda hostie [offe]rebantur", and "Sed quomodo sperat peccator quod habuit ill[e qui sine] peccato erit? bene".
    • The red signs stand where the edition prints its bracketed source labels: "c" at "Secunda pars" ([Cassiod.]), "ac" at "Sed quomodo sperat" and at "Quia apud Dominum est misericordia" ([Aug.] at both).
    • Alternatives considered:
    • The Glossa ordinaria on the Psalms (PL 113) has the same extracts in shorter form, abbreviated with "etc. usque ad". On verse 6 it gives "Speret vigilia matutina qui non aliud ... immortalitatem speret" before "caput et cauda hostiae". The fragment, like Lombard, puts "caput et cauda" first and the immortality sentence after it. The Glossa also lacks the fragment's "Sed quomodo sperat peccator".
    • Augustine's Enarrationes on Ps 129 and Alcuin's Enchiridion are sources the commentary draws on, in other wording, not the containing work.
    • The anonymous commentary on the seven penitential psalms printed among Innocent III's works shares the "caput et cauda" motif in different words.
    • Gilbert of Poitiers' commentary on the Psalms, which is unprinted and on which Lombard drew, was not compared.
    • Differences from PL 191:
    • "Qua spe clamas" for "Quia spe clamat";
    • "iniquitates" for "iniquitatem";
    • "In hoc est mihi spes" for "In hoc mihi est spes";
    • "sanguis innocens Christi" for "innocens sanguis Christi";
    • "ubi laeta conciliatio" for "ubi est laeta conclusio";
    • "non est enim castum cor quod de iusticia sua presumat" for "et non est castum cor quod praesumat de justitia sua";
    • "Unde subdit" added before the verse 4 lemma;
    • "hoc anima nostra c[oepit]" for "hoc coepit sperare anima nostra";
    • "salu[us erit] tamen(?)" and an abbreviated word read "h~n~", not "Inde", before "in lege" for "salvus erit tantum. Inde in lege";
    • "usquequo moriamur" without "non";
    • "offerebantur" for "offerebatur";
    • "similiter Israel speret" for "similiter speret Israel";
    • "qui sine peccato erit" for "qui sine peccato erat";
    • probably "totius peccata [mundi]" for "totius mundi peccata".

"Qua spe clamas", "Unde subdit" and "offerebantur" are also the readings of the Glossa ordinaria. Before the Psalm 130 initial there is room for only about two lines after "[vend]ere potuit". PL still has about four to five lines' worth of text at that point (the end of the Augustine sentence and a closing Cassiodorus sentence). The text in the manuscript is therefore shorter here, but the lines are rubbed and whether the Cassiodorus sentence is shortened or missing cannot be read.

  • Transcription: the image-only first reading and the source-assisted reading.
    • A research reader reread all four piece-faces on the native images. The two upside-down strips were read rotated, and the verse 4 and verse 6 lines were read again at higher zoom with the edition in hand.
    • An independent checker reread the lower half of the larger piece on canvas 1 and its tab (Ps 129:7 to the Ps 130 incipit), as described under Verification.
    • The lowest lines before the Ps 130 initial and the first line of the tab on canvas 0 are rubbed and only partly read.

It compared the remaining lines of verses 3, 6-8 with the same edition, first in its archive.org text and then on the page images.
    • An independent checker read a passage the research reader had relied on least, the lower lines of the larger piece on canvas 1 and its tab, on the image, and compared it with the page image of PL 191, col. 1170. It found agreement through "redimet [Israel]" and the Ps 130 incipit. It also found the word-order difference "totius peccata [mundi]" and the red "c" beside "sanguis pretiosus", where PL has no source label.     • The coordinator compared canvas 1, piece B, lines 6-11 (native box 4600,2450,5720,2850, "[Aug.] quia apud dominum misericordia quia ille qui sine peccato precessit ... ipse qui sine peccato potuit redimere", Ps 129:7-8) with PL 191 col. 1170 on the page image (archive.org n590): the wording agrees in sequence.

Differences. Besides those listed under Identification:

  • the fragment adds "est" in "quia apud te est propitiatio";
  • "est" is written above the line in "Quia est in eo misericordia";
  • the fragment has no "et" in "quod in nobis speramus";
  • the word before "ita iunge" looks like "Vt" and is not the "Vel" of PL; whether it is a shortened "vel" is uncertain;
  • the word before "in lege" (PL "Inde") is an abbreviation read "h~n~" whose expansion is uncertain.

Where the fragment and PL differ and the Glossa ordinaria (PL 113) has the same passage, the fragment three times has the Glossa's reading: "Qua spe clamas", "unde subdit", "offerebantur". The red "c" beside "sanguis pretiosus" has no counterpart label in PL. A red curl at the upper left of the tab, where a red hairline from the top of the initial ends, may be the end of the initial's stroke or another "c"; it cannot be told. If it is a "c", it would mark a Cassiodorus extract in the two rubbed lines before the Ps 130 initial.

These differences show that the fragment and the printed text differ. They do not show how the fragment's text relates to the manuscripts behind the edition.

Leaf order, other items and limits

The fragment is two pieces of one leaf, a strip and a larger piece with a tab, cut from one column. Which face was the recto is not established. Between the two faces about verse 4 (end) and verse 5 of the commentary are missing, roughly 1,300 characters of the printed text.

The large red initial on the tab is a round (uncial) D: its bowl is closed, so it looks like an O at a glance, and a red hairline runs from the top of the bowl to the left margin. It is followed by "NE" in brown capitals (the N shaped like an H), giving the contraction D(omi)NE, and the next line has "s(unt) oculi". It opens Psalm 130 and shows that the psalm text began with a decorated initial. No other items are in the images. The pencil notes "Stams, STA, Frag. 146-1r" and "146-1v" are modern holding marks.

Not claimed:

  • the date or origin of the manuscript (the script suggests the second half of the twelfth century, but this was not examined further);
  • which version or manuscript family of Lombard's commentary the fragment follows;
  • whether Gilbert of Poitiers' commentary shares these passages;
  • what the two rubbed lines before the Ps 130 initial contained.
