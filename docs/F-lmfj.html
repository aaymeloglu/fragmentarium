Manuscript and reading scope

Fragmentarium record and images; IIIF manifest. Canvas 0 is fol. [5]r and canvas 1 fol. [5]v. The catalogue description calls this an unidentified medical text with gloss, dated 1351–1400, in Northern Textualis, possibly English: one bound parchment leaf, 292 × 206 mm. These dating and origin statements are catalogue information, not independently established here.

The leaf has two columns. A large script for the Hippocratic lemma alternates with smaller commentary in the same column. The recto running title reads REG / ACUT. This layout, title, and the two independently compared text layers support describing it as a lemma-and-commentary witness. The verso is soiled; viewing it does not establish a complete reading of its obscured text.

The selected excerpts use physical column numbering: the short note at r.a20 is counted separately before the large lemma begins at r.a21. Parentheses contain proposed abbreviation expansions, [?] marks a doubtful reading, [...] unread text, and {above: ...} an interlinear addition. Ordinary u/v forms are used; these are expanded readings, not facsimiles of every abbreviation sign. Line breaks and column transitions are retained.

Edition comparison and differences

The directly inspected comparison source is the NLM copy of the Articella printed at Venice by Philippus Pincius in 1491. Its item metadata explicitly describes De regimine with Galen's commentary. On image n181, headed Liber re. acu., the Hec autem lemma occupies the broad right column, followed below by Plurimum autem. The preceding commentary on labour and digestion appears in the narrow far-right column above Iam aggregavit. The agreement of sustained prose in both commentary passages supports identification with the text printed there as Galen's commentary; the claim does not rest on the running title or a medical commonplace alone.

CheckManuscript and edition evidence
Lemma orderThe manuscript places Oportet ... ab usu sui before ad furie quoque ... sicut dicturus sum. The print puts the ad furie clause before Oportet. Both blocks are present, but their order differs.
Lemma wordingManuscript r.b2 has ab usu sui; the print has ab usu cibi. Calling this a scribal error would require further evidence.
CorrectionManuscript r.a26 adds sit above the line after ut. Its placement is retained rather than represented as an ordinary baseline word.
Commentary wordingManuscript r.b10 has de eo where the print has de ea. At r.b18 the manuscript has dispositio una, whereas the print includes una sua.
Diagnostic sequencePlurimum autem sermonis mei leads through repletion/evacuation, illnesses, sorbition, and vacuitatem venarum in both witnesses; the preceding bodily-labour discussion and following Iam aggregavit passage also correspond.

These agreements establish the work and commentary identification despite local textual differences. They do not determine a translator, an exact recension, the source of the transposition, or a manuscript's dependence on another witness.

Actual limits and remaining work

The image comparison used original-detail display of the recto and verso full images, upper and lower right recto crops, an upper left verso crop, two recto-left tiles, and the actual 1491 edition image. Cached 1491 and 1519 OCR were searched, without recovering reliable diagnostic passages. No 1519 page was collated, and no conclusion depends on an OCR no-hit. The Fragmentarium overview and the 1519 item page did not load through the web tool; the cached manuscript images were available.

Completing the verso, settling the corrections at r.a16 and r.b3, and assigning exact section endpoints require further manuscript and edition comparison. No precise Arabic-Latin translation layer, exhaustive exclusion of other commentators, or contents of a following leaf is established.
