Readings and comparison details

F-gcgt: Anonymous (attributed in some manuscripts to William Heytesbury), Termini naturales, from the middle-degree rule to the figures of bodies

The leaf carries part of the English school glossary of natural philosophy that was printed, without an author's name, as the "Tractatus de naturalibus" in the Libellus sophistarum ad usum Oxoniensium. That tract is a form of the Termini naturales (Thorndike and Kibre, col. 901), a short list of defined terms of physics which some manuscripts attribute to William Heytesbury. The fragment runs from the rule that a uniformly difform quality corresponds to its middle degree, through the definitions of motion and of the intension and remission of motion, the four causes and the three principles of nature, to the shapes of bodies. Its wording is close to the print but not identical, and which manuscript version of the work it represents is not determined.

The Fragmentarium record describes Gent, Universiteitsbibliotheek, HS.2583/199 as one trimmed parchment leaf, 196 x 139 mm, in a cursive script, from England, dated 1401-1450, with the title "Treatise on latitude and movement". The title fits the upper part of the text (latitude is the scholastic term for the range of degrees through which a quality or a speed can vary) but not the rest of the leaf, which goes on to causes and geometry. The record names no author or work. The date, origin and script are the catalogue's statements and were not examined here.

  • Identification: identified, high confidence, as the text printed as "Tractatus de naturalibus" in the Oxford Libellus sophistarum, a form of the Termini naturales. The basis is agreement in wording and in sequence across all four columns, including three changes of subject (qualities to motion, motion to causes, causes to figures) and the passage from one face to the other. The definitions of uniform and difform motion are common to many texts of the Oxford school of the fourteenth century, and the definition of the material cause with the example of timber and stones for a house also appears in commentaries on Aristotle's Physics printed under Thomas Aquinas's name; neither passage alone would identify the work, and the identification rests on the whole run of text. The fragment differs from the print: it divides motion, intension and remission first in two and then in two again where the print has a fourfold division, it lacks several sentences the print has, and it is fuller in one place (details below). The work circulated in more than one version, among them a redaction by Richard Lavenham; the fragment was compared only with the 1510 print, so its version is left open. The attribution to Heytesbury is found in some manuscripts and is treated as uncertain in the scholarship; other manuscripts name other masters.
  • Transcription: the image-only first reading was made before any source was known. The source-assisted reading was made by a research reader with the first reading and the print at hand: it covers the lower two thirds of the first column of the verso, and the other three columns entire. The stained upper third of the first column of the verso rests on the first reading alone. The numeral before "modis" in the divisions of motion, intension and remission is uncertain and is marked so. Accuracy has not been measured.
  • Verification: a research reader compared five manuscript passages, read on crops of the library's images, with page images of the 1510 print (scan images 101 and 102); the scan's machine-read text was used only to find the pages. COORDINATOR CHECK: the coordinator (Claude) read the recto, column b, top (canvas 0, box 2330,700,3900,1000) on the image against scan image 101 of the 1510 print: the four causes with the house example follow the print in order, and the leaf is fuller at "a nocumentis exterioribus ab ymbribus caloribus et frigoribus" where the print has "a frigore et a tempestate". Two further readers, working from shuffled crops without the source, read the latitudo-motus passages of the verso to the same effect.. Gaps: the print's lines for the uniformly difform intension are poorly inked in the scan and partly out of order, and were read with the help of the surrounding definitions; the end of the printed tract was read in the scan's machine-read text, not on the image; the manuscript catalogues and the scholarship cited for the attribution were seen as search excerpts, not read on the page; the modern editions of the Termini naturales and of Lavenham's redaction were not consulted. No human palaeographic review is claimed.

Differences. The print divides motion, and after it intension and remission, "quadrupliciter" (uniform, difform, uniformly difform, difformly difform). The manuscript divides each into uniform and difform and then divides difform in two; the numeral it writes before "modis" could be read as 3 or as 2 and is left uncertain. The manuscript lacks the print's sentence "Unde mouere difformiter est plus de spacio ...", the end of the definition of the formal cause ("qua posita ponitur et res, qua ablata aufertur et res"), the sentence on the privation of form with the example of a knife, and the transitional heading "Modo restat dicere de figuris corporum"; the definition of the efficient cause would fall in the trimmed top line of the column and cannot be judged. Where the print names the intrinsic and the extrinsic causes, the surviving manuscript text names the extrinsic pair. The manuscript is fuller in the final cause of the house ("a nocumentis exterioribus ab ymbribus tempestatibus et frigoribus et huiusmodi") and reads "figura resultans ex coniunctione" where the print has "forma resultans ex conuentione". It reads "per latitudinem adquiritur spacium" where the print has "per motum", which looks like a slip. There are small differences of wording and order throughout (fit / componitur, eque velociter / equaliter, finalis et efficiens / efficiens et finalis). These show that the two texts differ; they do not show which form is earlier or how the fragment's copy relates to the print's.

Source. Libellus sophistarum ad usum Oxoniensium, London, Wynkyn de Worde (named on the title page; the Internet Archive scan is dated 1510 in its description, and the exact edition was not established): "Tractatus de naturalibus", scan images 101 and 102. The tract opens at scan image 92 with "Natura est duplex scilicet natura naturans et natura naturata ..." and, a few lines on, "Natura est principium motus et quietis eius in quo est primo et principaliter per se et non per accidens". It ends, just after the figures of bodies, with "Corpus planum est cuius superficies est plana et nullatenus acuta, ut tabula".

Attribution. The print names no author. Thorndike and Kibre, col. 901, record the Termini naturales with the opening "Natura est principium motus et quietis eius in quo est primo et per se", and manuscript catalogues give its close as a definition of the flat body ending "ut tabula". The printed tract has that close and has that opening definition after a short preface on natura naturans, which is the ground for calling it a form of the same work; the opening definition alone would not be enough, since it is Aristotle's and begins other lists of terms as well. Catalogues of manuscripts attribute the Termini naturales to William Heytesbury in some copies (for example at Worcester Cathedral) and to other masters in others (one Oxford college copy ends "secundum M. Johannem Garisdale"). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on Heytesbury lists the work among those of uncertain authenticity and notes that it survives in several versions. A record of the earlier Pynson edition of the Libellus is reported to query Thomas Walsingham as author of the tract; that record was not read. The author is therefore given here as anonymous, with the Heytesbury attribution as some manuscripts' statement.

Leaf order, and what is not claimed

The text passes from the foot of the second column of the face labelled fol. [1]v to the head of the first column of the face labelled fol. [1]r, as the third row of the table shows. The leaf is to be read verso first; the catalogue's recto is the later page.

The lower third of each face is blank apart from library stamps and a faint mark; a short marginal note between the columns of the catalogue's recto was not read. A large brown stain from paste covers the first column and the top of the second on the catalogue's verso.

Not claimed: the date and origin of the copy, which are the catalogue's; the version of the Termini naturales the fragment represents, and its relation to the printed form or to Lavenham's redaction; the authorship of the work; whether this copy is otherwise known to scholarship.
