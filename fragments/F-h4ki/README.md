# F-h4ki: Office lessons from the homily "Surdus ille et mutus" (Mark 7:31-37) and a Luke 18 homily, with responsories of the Job historia

The strip is made of scraps from an Office book with lessons and responsories (a breviary or a similar book; the kind is not established). Its large-script scraps carry lessons from two homilies that Patrologia Latina 94 prints one after the other for successive Sundays: the end of a passage on the Pharisee and the publican (Luke 18:12) and, under the red rubric "Omelia v. b[...]", the opening and a later passage of the homily "Surdus ille et mutus" on Mark 7:31-37. Its small-script scrap carries the Matins responsory "Numquid Dominus supplantat iudicium" of the Job historia (the chants sung with the readings from Job, from late summer) on one face, and on the other face probably "Versa est in luctum cithara mea" of the same historia.

The [Fragmentarium record](https://fragmentarium.ms/overview/F-h4ki) is Stams, Stiftsarchiv, Frg. 258: parchment, 253 x 66 mm, restored, no date given, title "x". The catalogue identifies nothing; its description as one fragment item fits a strip that is in fact several scraps mounted on repair tissue.

- **Identification:** identified, high confidence for the texts; the kind of book is not established. The diagnostic points are (1) the red rubric "Omelia . v . b[...]" beside a red S and "[S]urdus ille et", the incipit of the homily (read also by a blind reader as "Omelia . v . be[...]" and "-s ille"); (2) three successive line beginnings, read with the source as "custodia(m) / q(uo)d co(n)t(r)a [h]ostiu(m) insid[ias] / tota", which stand at the predicted line intervals in the Luke 18 homily printed before "Surdus ille" in PL 94 (290 B); the image-only first reading, made before any source was proposed, has the compatible letter groups "custodia", "q(uo)d' com(m)i | a ostr(?) / osti(?) ... fid[...]" and "tota(?) / toti(?)", and the fuller wording is a reconstruction made with the source; (3) on the other face line ends read with the source as "seorsu(m)" and "[au]riculas" where the homily's text predicts them, at the back of the Luke scraps (291 B-C); the first reading has "u(er)su / uersu(m)(?)" and "[.]rticulas" there; and (4) the junction of Job 8:3 with Job 17:3 across a line break, which only the responsory "Numquid Dominus" has. The words "egrot[um]" and "[te]tigit li[nguam]" on the other face were read only with the source in view and a blind reader did not confirm them, so they are not used as evidence. The Luke 18 sentence is Gregory the Great's (Homilies on Ezekiel), taken over by Bede; the three part-lines cannot exclude Gregory's text, and the label "Bede" for that lesson rests on its place before the "Surdus ille" rubric. Psalm 140:3 ("pone custodiam ori meo et ostium") was considered for (3) and does not fit "qd" before and "insid" after. The Luke 18 passage stands in the same words in Bede's commentary on Luke and in the homily collections of Hrabanus Maurus and Smaragdus, so the three part-lines do not show from which collection this lesson was drawn. Several readings rest on damaged letters and are marked in the table. No musical notation is clearly visible, but small strokes above the chant lines may be faint staffless neumes, so the book may have been noted.

## Arrangement and what is not claimed

The faded red letters on the upper left of 01v are the 01r rubric showing through the parchment (mirrored, they read "Omelia . v . b"), not a second rubric. This fixes which piece backs which: the Mark 7:33 passage on 01v is the back of the Luke 18 scraps on 01r, and the mounting keeps the scraps in their relative positions. The lesson scraps and the responsory scrap are separate pieces of parchment divided by a horizontal seam; the responsory scrap is not in its original position on the mount, so it probably comes from the same book, but not shown to be the same leaf. The expansion "venerabilis Bede" for "v. b[...]" is an expansion; the name is cut after b. Not claimed: the kind of book (breviary, homiliary with chants, or noted Office book), the date or origin of the book, its use, the Sunday numbers it gave the two homilies, or the identity of the item that ends before "Versa est".
